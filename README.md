2.1 Overview and Historical Significance
The Venice Lagoon is the largest lagoon in the Mediterranean Sea, covering approximately
550 km2. It is a shallow coastal ecosystem formed by the interaction of fresh water from the
drainage basins of the Brenta, Sile, and Piave rivers with the saline waters of the Adriatic Sea,
connecting to the open sea through three inlets: Lido, Malamocco, and Chioggia. The lagoon
has been inhabited since the 5th century AD and has sustained one of the most remarkable
urban civilisations in human history.
Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, the lagoon and its historic city are subject
to strict national and international regulations governing any physical intervention in the
aquatic environment. This regulatory framework is a primary constraint for any energy
harvesting device designed for deployment in this context.
2.2 Hydrodynamic Characteristics
2.2.1 Bathymetry and Water Depth
The morphology of the lagoon is characterised by an extremely shallow basin. The average
water depth is between 1 and 2 metres, punctuated by deeper navigation channels (up to 8–
12 m in the main shipping lanes) and by vast tidal flats that emerge above the water surface
at low tide. This shallow morphology has profound consequences for the selection of energy
harvesting technologies: conventional submerged current turbines require minimum flow
velocities and depths that the Venice Lagoon generally cannot provide.
2.2.2 Tidal Regime and Acqua Alta
Venice experiences a semi-diurnal micro-tidal regime with a relatively small vertical tidal
range, typically between 50 and 100 centimetres under normal meteorological conditions. The
phenomenon of Acqua Alta occurs when a combination of astronomical tides, storm surges,
and adverse wind patterns (particularly the Scirocco and Bora winds) causes water levels to
exceed 80 cm above the Punta della Dogana reference level, flooding the lower-lying areas
of the city including Piazza San Marco.
The frequency and intensity of Acqua Alta events have increased dramatically over the past
century, driven by both the global rise in mean sea level and local land subsidence. This makes
real-time water level monitoring — one of the primary missions of a sensor network powered
by the Nautilus Harvester — an environmental and civic priority of the highest order.
2.2.3 Current Velocity

Direct velocity measurements across the open areas of the lagoon record average tidal-
induced flow velocities in the range of 0.04 to 0.06 m/s (4 to 6 cm/s). Near the seabed, due to

viscous friction and the presence of dense marine vegetation (primarily Zostera marina and
Cymodocea nodosa), velocities approach zero. Near the primary tidal inlets, velocities can
exceed 1 m/s during spring tides.

2.2.4 Wave Characteristics and Moto Ondoso
Wind-generated waves in the Venice Lagoon are characterised by high frequency and low
amplitude — a consequence of the extremely limited fetch (the maximum distance over which
wind can act) imposed by the shallow basin. Peak significant wave heights under storm
conditions rarely exceed 0.5 m in the open lagoon.
Of far greater practical importance for energy harvesting is the wave field generated by the
passage of motorised vessels — a phenomenon referred to locally as moto ondoso, or
motorboat-induced wake wash. The Venetian lagoon is served by a dense network of public
and private watercraft, including the iconic vaporetti (water buses), water taxis, gondolas,
delivery barges, and emergency vessels. The wakes generated by these vessels, particularly
the vaporetti on their regular routes, create periodic wave pulses with frequencies and
amplitudes well matched to the resonance characteristics of a pendulum-type harvester.
2.3 Water Quality
The water of the Venice Lagoon is brackish, with salinity typically ranging from 20 to 36 PSU
depending on proximity to the river inlets and tidal exchanges. It carries high concentrations
of suspended particulate matter (silt and clay), and is subject to significant seasonal variations
in temperature (from approximately 4°C in winter to 28°C in summer). These conditions
accelerate electrochemical corrosion of metallic components and promote biological fouling
(biofouling) on any submerged or semi-submerged structure.
The design of any device intended for long-term deployment in this environment must
therefore incorporate appropriate material selection (stainless steel, HDPE, marine-grade
aluminium, sealed polymers), surface treatments (antifouling coatings, anodisation), and
modular accessibility to facilitate maintenance without requiring specialised diving equipment.
2.4 Solar Resource
Venice benefits from a Mediterranean-influenced climate, receiving between 2,000 and 2,100
hours of sunshine per year. This corresponds approximately to 100–120 fully sunny days, 80–
100 predominantly rainy or overcast days, and 140–160 days of mixed or partly cloudy
conditions annually.
Practical implications for a solar-powered buoy include:
• Summer conditions: peak solar irradiance of up to 1,000 W/m2, with 5+ effective peak
sun hours per day.
• Winter conditions: effective peak sun hours may drop to as low as 1–1.5 hours per
day during overcast periods.
• Dense fog (nebbia): Venice is famous for prolonged periods of dense winter fog that
effectively reduce solar irradiance to near-zero for multiple consecutive days.

2.5 The MOSE System
The Modulo Sperimentale Elettromeccanico (MOSE) is a major hydraulic infrastructure project
consisting of 78 mobile flap barriers installed at the three lagoon inlets (Lido, Malamocco, and
Chioggia). When deployed, the barriers rise from their housing caissons on the seabed and
isolate the lagoon from the Adriatic, preventing extreme tidal flooding events.
The Nautilus Harvester must be designed and deployed in strict compliance with the
operational envelope of the MOSE system. This means that no device component may be
anchored to the bottom in proximity to the MOSE caissons, and the buoy's mooring system
must be designed to avoid entanglement with or interference with the barrier structures and
their associated sensor and communication infrastructure.
